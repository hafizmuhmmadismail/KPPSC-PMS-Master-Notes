### EXECUTIVE EXAM METADATA
- **EXAM QUESTIONS LINKED:** 2022 Q.1: ''Revolutions often start from the streets but eventually get completed in legislatures.' In the light of this statement, assess the role of the National Assembly in France between 1789 and 1791.'
- **SYLLABUS TOPIC:** Paper I — Section 1: The French Revolution — National Constituent Assembly & Constitutional Transformation (1789–1791)
- **ALLOCATED MARKS / TIME:** 20 Marks | 35 Minutes | Standard Word Count: ~650–700 words
- **HISTORIOGRAPHICAL THESIS:** While insurrectionary street mobilizations in Paris and peasant jacqueries in the countryside broke the coercive apparatus of Bourbon absolutism, it was the legislative craftsmanship of the National Constituent Assembly (1789–1791) that translated raw popular violence into an enduring institutional, administrative, and constitutional reality, proving that while streets dismantle absolute tyranny, legislatures institutionalize modern freedom.

### 📌 EXAM ESSENTIAL TAKEAWAY
> • The Street-Legislature Dialectic: Popular insurrections (Storming of the Bastille on July 14, Great Fear in the countryside in July–August, Women's March on Versailles on October 5–6) repeatedly rescued the bourgeois National Assembly from royal military repression and forced Louis XVI to yield.
>
> • The Legislative Miracle: In two years, the National Assembly dismantled over a millennium of feudal institutions: Night of August 4 (abolition of feudal dues and seigneurial courts); Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen (August 26, 1789); Civil Constitution of the Clergy (July 1790); and the Constitution of 1791.
>
> • Rationalized Administrative Architecture: Eradicated the chaotic 300-custom patchwork of the Ancien Régime, dividing France into 83 uniform, self-governing Départements; standardized weights and measures; eliminated internal customs barriers; and instituted trial by jury.
>
> • Bourgeois Contradiction & Limits: The Assembly restricted the franchise through property qualifications, dividing citizens into 'Active' (tax-paying men with voting rights) and 'Passive' (excluded working classes), sowing the seeds for future radical Jacobin insurrections.
>

### 3. THE 20-MARK MASTER MODEL ESSAY (~650 WORDS)

#### Introduction: The Street-Legislature Dialectic
The axiom that 'revolutions often start from the streets but eventually get completed in legislatures' encapsulates the essential dialectic of the French Revolution between 1789 and 1791. Left to themselves, street insurrections and peasant jacqueries represent destructive energies capable of paralyzing an authoritarian regime, yet inherently incapable of constructing a durable institutional order. Conversely, legislative bodies operating in a political vacuum are easily intimidated or dispersed by armed royalist power. Between the summer of 1789 and the autumn of 1791, the French National Constituent Assembly functioned as the institutional crucible of the modern world precisely because it engaged in a symbiotic dialogue with popular street mobilization. While the storming of the Bastille and the October Days shattered the crown's coercive apparatus, it was the jurists, philosophers, and bourgeois deputies of the National Assembly who forged that raw rebellious fury into the constitutional, administrative, and legal foundation of the modern French state.

#### The Streets as the Armed Protector of the Legislature
The National Assembly was born in defiance on June 17, 1789, when the Third Estate, joined by liberal clerics and nobles, proclaimed itself the sovereign representative body of France and took the solemn Tennis Court Oath. However, King Louis XVI immediately concentrated twenty thousand Swiss and German mercenary troops around Paris to crush the nascent parliament. The Assembly was preserved from military dissolution exclusively by the spontaneous intervention of the streets. On July 14, 1789, Parisian artisans, shopkeepers, and wage-earners stormed the Bastille fortress, seizing thirty thousand muskets and lynching Governor de Launay. This street uprising permanently broke the royal monopoly on violence, forced Louis XVI to withdraw his troops, accept the tricolor cockade, and legitimize the National Assembly. Similarly, when rural peasants ignited the 'Great Fear' (Grande Peur) in July and August by burning châteaux and seigneurial debt-registers, the Assembly was compelled to accelerate structural reform. Furthermore, during the October Days of 1789, an armed crowd of Parisian market women marched twelve miles through torrential rain to Versailles, storming the palace and compelling both the royal family and the National Assembly to relocate to Paris under the watchful eye of the sovereign populace.

#### The Legislative Masterstrokes: Dismantling Feudalism and Codifying Rights
Having been shielded by popular force, the National Assembly executed an astonishing legislative revolution. In the historic nocturnal session of August 4, 1789, liberal aristocrats and deputies renounced their ancestral privileges, formally abolishing the feudal regime in its entirety. Feudal personal servitude, seigneurial jurisdictions, aristocratic hunting monopolies, and ecclesiastical tithes were obliterated overnight, establishing absolute legal equality. Three weeks later, on August 26, 1789, the Assembly promulgated the 'Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.' Drafted by the Marquis de Lafayette with input from Thomas Jefferson, this monumental document codified Enlightenment philosophy into positive law: it affirmed that men are born free and equal in rights, declared private property sacred and inviolable, established liberty, security, and resistance to oppression as fundamental entitlements, and proclaimed that sovereignty resides not in the dynastic person of the king, but essentially in the Nation.

#### Administrative, Judicial, and Fiscal Restructuring
The constructive genius of the National Assembly was most vividly displayed in its comprehensive rationalization of French governance. The archaic, labyrinthine administrative patchwork of bailiwicks, intendancies, and custom zones was swept away and replaced by eighty-three uniform, geographically balanced departments (départements), subdivided into districts and communes, each governed by locally elected councils. In the judicial sphere, hereditary venal magistrate offices were abolished, torture was outlawed, and a unified secular legal system featuring trial by public juries was introduced. To resolve the catastrophic royal debt, the Assembly nationalized all Roman Catholic Church property on November 2, 1789 (biens nationaux), issuing interest-bearing bonds known as assignats. While the assignats eventually succumbed to hyperinflation due to over-issuance, the sale of church lands permanently tied the peasant and bourgeois purchasers to the survival of the revolutionary settlement.

#### Contradictions, Fractures, and the Bourgeois Limitation
Despite its monumental achievements, the National Assembly suffered from fatal contradictions that ultimately undermined its moderate constitutional vision. Guided by bourgeois economic interests, the Assembly passed the Le Chapelier Law (June 1791), which outlawed trade unions and collective strikes, leaving urban wage-earners defenseless against capitalist exploitation. More critically, the Constitution of 1791 betrayed the egalitarian promise of the Declaration of Rights by dividing Frenchmen into 'Active' and 'Passive' citizens. Only affluent men paying direct taxes equivalent to three days of labor were granted voting rights, disenfranchising over three million working-class citizens (sans-culottes). The Assembly's most catastrophic miscalculation, however, was the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (July 1790). By transforming priests into salaried civil servants elected by parishioners and demanding a loyalty oath to the state, the Assembly alienated the Pope, split the French clergy into 'juring' and 'refractory' camps, and ignited a bitter religious civil war across rural France (notably in the Vendée).

#### Conclusion: The Triumph and Legacy of the Legislative Crucible
In conclusion, the history of France between 1789 and 1791 affirms that revolutions require both the disruptive eruption of the street and the institutional codification of the legislature. Without the storming of the Bastille, the Tennis Court oath would have ended in mass arrests; without the Assembly's systematic drafting of eighty-three departments, the abolition of feudalism, and the Constitution of 1791, the street uprisings would have dissipated into aimless anarchy. Although the Flight to Varennes in June 1791 exposed the impossibility of a constitutional monarchy under Louis XVI and propelled France toward the radical Jacobin Republic, the institutional scaffolding erected by the National Constituent Assembly proved permanent. The administrative, legal, and egalitarian framework codified between 1789 and 1791 survived the Reign of Terror, Napoleon Bonaparte, and the Bourbon Restoration, enduring as the bedrock of modern democratic France.
